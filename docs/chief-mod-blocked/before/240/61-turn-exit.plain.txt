  The History of Tea

  Origins in the Mists

  Tea begins as a plant before it becomes a drink, and the plant begins in the uplands where southwest China, northern Myanmar, and northeastern India meet. Camellia sinensis, an evergreen shrub of the camellia family, grew wild in these
  humid, forested hills long before anyone steeped its leaves. Botanists recognize two main varieties. C. sinensis var. sinensis is a small-leaved, cold-tolerant bush that became the basis of Chinese tea. C. sinensis var. assamica is a
  large-leaved tree that thrives in tropical warmth and later became the foundation of Indian Assam tea.

  The legends that surround tea's discovery are charming, and almost certainly not history. The most famous credits the mythical emperor Shennong, the "Divine Farmer," who is said to have tested hundreds of herbs on his own body. According
  to the tale, he was boiling water around 2737 BCE when leaves from a nearby tree drifted into his pot. He drank the infusion and found it refreshing, and it neutralized the poisons he had absorbed that day. A second legend, from Buddhist
  tradition, tells of Bodhidharma, who cut off his own eyelids in frustration after falling asleep during meditation. Where the eyelids fell, tea plants sprouted. These stories tell us less about the first drinkers than about how the
  Chinese and Buddhist traditions came to see tea: as medicine, and as an aid to wakefulness and discipline.

  The documentary evidence is more modest and more interesting. Archaeologists have found tea remains in the tomb of Emperor Jing of Han, who died in 141 BCE, near Xi'an. Chemical analysis in 2016 identified tea leaves among the grave
  goods, which suggests that by the second century BCE tea was valued enough to accompany an emperor into the afterlife. Earlier references are harder to pin down. The word often translated as "tea" in early texts, tu, seems to have covered
  several bitter herbs, and scholars still argue over which passages truly mean Camellia. Writers from the Han period mention tea drinking in the southwestern region of Sichuan, which probably was the center of early cultivation. A text
  from around 59 BCE, Wang Bao's "Contract for a Youth," lists the purchase of tea and the preparation of tea among a servant's duties. That is some of the earliest reliable evidence of tea as a commodity.
