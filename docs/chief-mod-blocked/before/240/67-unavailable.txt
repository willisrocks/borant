  [1;3;4mThe History of Tea[0m

  [1mOrigins in the Mists[0m

  Tea begins as a plant before it becomes a drink, and the plant begins in the uplands where southwest China, northern Myanmar, and northeastern India meet. [3mCamellia sinensis[0m, an evergreen shrub of the camellia family, grew wild in these
  humid, forested hills long before anyone steeped its leaves. Botanists recognize two main varieties. [3mC. sinensis[0m var. [3msinensis[0m is a small-leaved, cold-tolerant bush that became the basis of Chinese tea. [3mC. sinensis[0m var. [3massamica[0m is a
  large-leaved tree that thrives in tropical warmth and later became the foundation of Indian Assam tea.

  The legends that surround tea's discovery are charming, and almost certainly not history. The most famous credits the mythical emperor Shennong, the "Divine Farmer," who is said to have tested hundreds of herbs on his own body. According
  to the tale, he was boiling water around 2737 BCE when leaves from a nearby tree drifted into his pot. He drank the infusion and found it refreshing, and it neutralized the poisons he had absorbed that day. A second legend, from Buddhist
  tradition, tells of Bodhidharma, who cut off his own eyelids in frustration after falling asleep during meditation. Where the eyelids fell, tea plants sprouted. These stories tell us less about the first drinkers than about how the
  Chinese and Buddhist traditions came to see tea: as medicine, and as an aid to wakefulness and discipline.

  The documentary evidence is more modest and more interesting. Archaeologists have found tea remains in the tomb of Emperor Jing of Han, who died in 141 BCE, near Xi'an. Chemical analysis in 2016 identified tea leaves among the grave
  goods, which suggests that by the second century BCE tea was valued enough to accompany an emperor into the afterlife. Earlier references are harder to pin down. The word often translated as "tea" in early texts, [3mtu[0m, seems to have covered
  several bitter herbs, and scholars still argue over which passages truly mean [3mCamellia[0m. Writers from the Han period mention tea drinking in the southwestern region of Sichuan, which probably was the center of early cultivation. A text
  from around 59 BCE, Wang Bao's "Contract for a Youth," lists the purchase of tea and the preparation of tea among a servant's duties. That is some of the earliest reliable evidence of tea as a commodity.

  [1mTang Dynasty: Tea Becomes a Culture[0m

  Tea moved from regional curiosity to national habit in the Tang dynasty (618–907). Several forces drove this. The Grand Canal and improved roads linked the tea-growing south to the northern capitals. Buddhism spread widely, and monks
  prized tea for staying alert through long meditation. A prosperous, cosmopolitan society had the leisure and the money to turn a drink into a pastime.

  The key figure of the age is Lu Yu (c. 733–804), an orphan raised in a Buddhist monastery who became a scholar and recluse. His [3mCha Jing[0m, the [3mClassic of Tea[0m, written around 760–780, is the world's first major treatise on the subject. In
  ten chapters it covers the plant's origins, the tools for making and drinking tea, the manufacturing process, the proper utensils, the boiling of water, the drinking of tea, its history, its regional varieties, and what could be omitted
  in a simple setting. Lu Yu insisted that tea be treated with care and attention, and he held opinions on everything from the best water (mountain spring water, then river water, then well water) to the shape of the kettle. In doing so he
  elevated tea preparation into something close to an art.

  Tang tea was quite unlike what we drink now. Leaves were steamed, pounded, molded into cakes, and dried. To serve, the cake was toasted, ground to powder, and boiled in a pot, often with salt, and sometimes with ginger, orange peel, or
  other flavorings. Lu Yu grumbled about additives, but they remained popular. The liquid was a thick, savory broth rather than a delicate infusion.
