  The documentary evidence is more modest and more interesting. Archaeologists have found tea remains in the tomb of Emperor Jing of Han, who died in 141 BCE, near Xi'an. Chemical analysis in 2016 identified tea leaves among the grave
  goods, which suggests that by the second century BCE tea was valued enough to accompany an emperor into the afterlife. Earlier references are harder to pin down. The word often translated as "tea" in early texts, tu, seems to have covered
  several bitter herbs, and scholars still argue over which passages truly mean Camellia. Writers from the Han period mention tea drinking in the southwestern region of Sichuan, which probably was the center of early cultivation. A text
  from around 59 BCE, Wang Bao's "Contract for a Youth," lists the purchase of tea and the preparation of tea among a servant's duties. That is some of the earliest reliable evidence of tea as a commodity.

  Tang Dynasty: Tea Becomes a Culture

  Tea moved from regional curiosity to national habit in the Tang dynasty (618–907). Several forces drove this. The Grand Canal and improved roads linked the tea-growing south to the northern capitals. Buddhism spread widely, and monks
  prized tea for staying alert through long meditation. A prosperous, cosmopolitan society had the leisure and the money to turn a drink into a pastime.

  The key figure of the age is Lu Yu (c. 733–804), an orphan raised in a Buddhist monastery who became a scholar and recluse. His Cha Jing, the Classic of Tea, written around 760–780, is the world's first major treatise on the subject. In
  ten chapters it covers the plant's origins, the tools for making and drinking tea, the manufacturing process, the proper utensils, the boiling of water, the drinking of tea, its history, its regional varieties, and what could be omitted
  in a simple setting. Lu Yu insisted that tea be treated with care and attention, and he held opinions on everything from the best water (mountain spring water, then river water, then well water) to the shape of the kettle. In doing so he
  elevated tea preparation into something close to an art.

  Tang tea was quite unlike what we drink now. Leaves were steamed, pounded, molded into cakes, and dried. To serve, the cake was toasted, ground to powder, and boiled in a pot, often with salt, and sometimes with ginger, orange peel, or
  other flavorings. Lu Yu grumbled about additives, but they remained popular. The liquid was a thick, savory broth rather than a delicate infusion.
