  during long sessions, and tea's caffeine served them well. Monasteries became centers of tea growing and tea
  knowledge, an association that persisted for more than a thousand years.

  Early tea was often prepared by pressing leaves into cakes, which could be transported and traded easily. These cakes
  were roasted, ground, and boiled, sometimes with onion, ginger, orange peel, or salt. The result was closer to a soup
  than to the delicate infusion we know today.

  The Tang Dynasty: Tea Becomes a Culture

  Tea became a national drink during the Tang dynasty (618–907). Peace, prosperity, a vibrant urban culture, and the
  Grand Canal, which allowed cheap long-distance transport, created a mass market. Poets praised tea, and drinking it
  became a mark of refinement.

  The pivotal figure of this era is Lu Yu (c. 733–804), an orphan raised by Buddhist monks who became known as the "Sage
  of Tea." His Cha Jing, or Classic of Tea, written around 760–780, is the first comprehensive monograph on tea. It
  describes how to grow tea, how to process leaves into cakes, which utensils to use, which waters make the best
  infusion, and how tea relates to the spirit of the drinker. Lu Yu insisted on careful preparation and argued against
  adding too many flavorings. His work gave tea a philosophy, in which attention to detail, harmony with nature, and
  moderation were bound together.
