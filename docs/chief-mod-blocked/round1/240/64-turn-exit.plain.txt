  The History of Tea: From Leaf to Global Empire

  Introduction

  Tea is the most widely consumed manufactured beverage in the world, second only to water in total volume. Behind each cup lies a history spanning more than four thousand years. It touches medicine, religion, diplomacy, war, commerce, and
  the daily rituals of nearly every culture on earth. Tea shaped empires and toppled them. It financed colonial ventures, sparked revolutions, and created entire industries of porcelain, silver, and shipping. To trace its history is to
  trace a thread through the larger story of human civilization.

  Origins in Ancient China

  The origins of tea lie in the mountainous southwest of China, in the region of modern Yunnan, Sichuan, and the borderlands of Myanmar and Vietnam. This is where Camellia sinensis, the evergreen shrub that produces all true tea, grows
  wild. Botanists generally agree that the plant is native to this area, and some ancient wild tea trees there are still living.

  Legend gives the discovery a more charming origin. According to Chinese tradition, in 2737 BCE the mythical emperor Shennong, the "Divine Farmer" who taught humanity agriculture and herbal medicine, was boiling water beneath a tree when
  leaves drifted into his pot. He sipped the infusion, found it refreshing, and tea was born. Shennong was said to have tested hundreds of herbs on himself, and tea served as an antidote to the poisons he encountered. The story is almost
  certainly apocryphal, since the earliest written accounts of Shennong's deeds appear many centuries after the supposed date. But it captures an essential truth about early tea: it was valued first as a medicine.

  Archaeological evidence suggests tea use is genuinely ancient. Remains of tea leaves found in the tomb of Emperor Jing of Han, who died in 141 BCE, indicate that the elite of the Han dynasty prized the plant. Early textual references,
  such as a contract from 59 BCE by the writer Wang Bao that mentions buying tea and preparing it, suggest tea was by then a commodity in Sichuan. At this stage tea was probably eaten and drunk in ways that would seem strange to modern
  drinkers. Leaves were boiled with onions, ginger, orange peel, and salt into something closer to a soup or a bitter herbal broth than the delicate beverage we know today.

  Tea Becomes a Culture: The Tang Dynasty

  Tea's transformation from regional medicine to national beverage occurred during the Tang dynasty (618–907 CE). Stability, prosperity, and the expansion of trade routes allowed tea to spread across China. Buddhism played a central role.
  Monks found that tea helped them stay alert during long hours of meditation, and monasteries became centers of tea cultivation and preparation. As Buddhism spread, so did tea drinking.
