  wild tree. A few leaves drifted into the pot. The emperor, curious about the fragrant liquid, drank it and found it
  invigorating. The tale is almost certainly apocryphal, but it captures something true: tea entered human life first as
  a medicine, and only later as a pleasure.

  The plant itself, Camellia sinensis, is native to the borderlands of southwest China, northern Myanmar, and
  northeastern India. Wild tea trees grew in the forested uplands of what is now Yunnan, and the peoples of the region
  likely chewed the leaves, cooked them into soups, or pickled them long before anyone steeped them in water. Some
  ethnic groups in the region, such as the Bulang and Dai, still eat fermented tea leaves. The earliest archaeological
  evidence of tea consumption is more concrete than the legend: tea residue has been identified in the tomb of Emperor
  Jing of Han, who died in 141 BCE, near Xi'an. That the emperor was buried with tea suggests that by the second century
  BCE it was valued enough to accompany a ruler into the afterlife.

  In these early centuries tea was a regional curiosity. Texts from the Han dynasty mention it as a medicinal drink, and
  by the third century CE it was being consumed in the Sichuan basin, where it was sometimes prepared with ginger,
  orange peel, and onion, more like a broth than the beverage we know. It was a long way from the refined ceremony it
  would become.
