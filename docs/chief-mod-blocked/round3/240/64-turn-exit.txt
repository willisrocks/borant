  [1;3;4mThe Leaf That Shaped the World: A History of Tea[0m

  [1mOrigins in Myth and Mountains[0m

  Tea begins in a landscape, not a teacup. The plant [3mCamellia sinensis[0m is an evergreen shrub native to the forested uplands where southwestern China, northern Myanmar, and northeastern India meet. For thousands of years before anyone
  steeped its leaves, it grew wild in the humid, misty hills of this region. Peoples such as the Bulang, the Wa, and the Dai lived alongside it, and some of them probably chewed the leaves, cooked them as a vegetable, or pickled them. Some
  of these practices survive. Myanmar's [3mlahpet[0m, a fermented tea-leaf salad, is a living reminder that tea was food before it was a drink.

  The Chinese tradition prefers a founding legend. It credits the mythical emperor Shennong, the "Divine Farmer," who is said to have tested hundreds of herbs on himself. According to the story, in about 2737 BCE a few leaves drifted into
  the water he was boiling. He drank the infusion and found it refreshing, and it neutralized the poisons he had absorbed in his experiments. The tale is charming and almost certainly invented. Shennong is a cultural symbol more than a
  historical person, and the earliest versions of the story appear many centuries after his supposed lifetime. Still, the legend tells us something true. From its earliest records, tea was bound up with medicine, vitality, and the idea of a
  drink that clarifies the mind.

  Physical evidence is more reliable than legend. In 2016, archaeologists reported tea residue in the tomb of Emperor Jing of Han, who died in 141 BCE, near modern Xi'an. Tea leaves had also reached Tibet's Ngari region by roughly the
  second century BCE. These finds suggest that by the Han dynasty (206 BCE to 220 CE), tea was valued enough to accompany an emperor into the afterlife. Written sources from this period are sparse, and some use ambiguous characters that may
  refer to other bitter plants. By the third and fourth centuries CE, however, texts clearly discuss tea drinking. The physician Hua Tuo is credited with the observation that drinking tea steadily improves thought, and the general Liu Kun
  asked relatives to send him tea to relieve his melancholy.

  In these early centuries tea was still a regional taste, centered in Sichuan and the Yangtze valley. It was prepared as a kind of soup or medicinal brew, often boiled with ginger, orange peel, onion, or salt. The refined, leaf-and-water
  minimalism we now associate with tea had not yet appeared.
