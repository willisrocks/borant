  Early tea was nothing like the drink we know. It was often boiled with onion, ginger, orange peel, or salt, producing something closer to a soup or a medicinal decoction. The leaves were frequently pressed into cakes for storage and
  transport, a form that would remain central to Chinese and Central Asian tea culture for more than a thousand years.

  The Tang Dynasty: Tea Becomes a Culture

  Tea's transformation from regional remedy to national beverage occurred during the Tang dynasty (618–907). Several forces converged. Buddhism, which flourished under the Tang, valued tea as an aid to meditation, since monks could stay
  alert through long hours of sitting without breaking their prohibition on intoxicants. Monasteries cultivated tea on their hillsides and spread the habit along pilgrimage routes. At the same time, the dynasty's peace, its canal networks,
  and its cosmopolitan cities created the commercial conditions for a mass market.

  The decisive figure of this era is Lu Yu (c. 733–804), an orphan raised in a monastery who became a scholar and connoisseur. His Cha Jing, or Classic of Tea, written around 760, is the world's first treatise on the subject. In three
  volumes and ten sections, Lu Yu described the plant, the tools for harvesting and processing it, the equipment for brewing, the best water to use, the methods of preparation, the history of tea, and the famous growing regions. He turned a
  commodity into an art and a discipline. His insistence that tea be prepared with care, that the water be neither too young nor too old, and that the vessels be harmonious with the drink established the idea that tea was not merely
  consumed but contemplated. Later generations would honor him as the "Sage of Tea."

  Tang tea was still prepared from compressed cakes. The cake was toasted, ground into powder, and simmered in a kettle with water, sometimes with a pinch of salt. The state recognized the commercial importance of the leaf: in 780, the
  government imposed the first tea tax, a harbinger of the fiscal importance tea would hold for governments all over the world.

  The Song Dynasty: Whisked Tea and Imperial Refinement

  Under the Song dynasty (960–1279), tea culture reached a remarkable level of sophistication. Powdered tea, whisked in a bowl until frothy, became the fashionable form. The finest tea cakes, stamped with dragons and phoenixes, were
  produced for the imperial court in the Beiyuan gardens of Fujian, and the competition to produce the most exquisite tribute tea became fierce. Aristocrats and literati held tea-tasting contests, called doucha, in which competitors judged
  the whiteness and persistence of the foam and the quality of the flavor. Black-glazed bowls from Fujian, with their lustrous streaks and spots, were prized because they showed off the white froth to best advantage.

  Emperor Huizong (r. 1100–1126), a gifted painter and calligrapher who proved a disastrous ruler, wrote a treatise on tea, the Daguan Chalun, in which he praised its power to bring tranquility and refinement. The cultural prestige of tea
  had by then reached the highest level of society, yet it also spread far down the social ladder. Teahouses proliferated in cities such as Kaifeng and Hangzhou, serving as places of conversation, business, entertainment, and sometimes less
  respectable pleasures.

  Tea also became a vital strategic commodity. The Song state, hard pressed by nomadic powers to the north and west, needed horses for its cavalry, and the steppe peoples and Tibetans wanted tea, which supplied vitamins and digestive relief
  to people subsisting on meat and dairy. The result was the Tea-Horse trade, a network of mountain trails through Sichuan and Yunnan into Tibet that would continue for centuries. Government monopolies regulated this exchange, and tea
  became, in effect, a form of currency in the frontier economy.
