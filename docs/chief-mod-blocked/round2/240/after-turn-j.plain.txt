  Origins in Southwest China                                                                                                                                   │
                                                                                                                                                               │
  The tea plant, Camellia sinensis, is native to the region where present-day Yunnan, Sichuan, Myanmar, and northern Laos and Vietnam meet. In these humid,    │
  forested uplands, wild tea trees grew for millennia before anyone thought to brew them. Exactly when people began to use the plant is lost to time, and the  │
  earliest chapters of tea history are bound up with legend.                                                                                                   │
                                                                                                                                                               │
  The best-known story credits the mythical emperor Shennong, the "Divine Farmer," who is said to have lived around 2737 BCE. According to the tale, Shennong  │
  tested herbs on himself to discover their medicinal properties, and on one occasion poisoned himself seventy-two times in a day. Leaves from a wild tree     │
  drifted into his boiling water, and the resulting infusion cured him. The story is charming, but it is a later invention, probably first written down more   │
  than two thousand years after its supposed date. Shennong belongs to myth, not to the historical record.                                                     │
                                                                                                                                                               │
  What the evidence does suggest is that tea was first used by the peoples of the southwest, perhaps chewed, eaten as a vegetable, or boiled into a bitter     │
  medicinal broth, long before it became a refined drink. Archaeological finds have complicated the picture further. Remains of tea leaves have been identified│
  in the tomb of Emperor Jing of Han, who died in 141 BCE, near Xi'an, which suggests the leaf was valued by the Han court by the second century BCE. Written  │
  references from the same era, though sometimes ambiguous because the Chinese character for tea was not yet standardized, point to tea as a drink in the      │
  Sichuan basin. A text from 59 BCE, a humorous contract by Wang Bao, mentions buying tea and preparing it, among the earliest reasonably secure references.   │
                                                                                                                                                               │
  Early tea was nothing like the drink we know. It was often boiled with onion, ginger, orange peel, or salt, producing something closer to a soup or a        │
  medicinal decoction. The leaves were frequently pressed into cakes for storage and transport, a form that would remain central to Chinese and Central Asian  │
  tea culture for more than a thousand years.                                                                                                                  │
