  [1;3;4mThe History of Tea: From Mountain Leaf to Global Commodity[0m

  [1mIntroduction[0m

  Few substances have shaped human civilization as quietly and as thoroughly as tea. It is a leaf, steeped in hot water, yet in its passage across continents it has built and toppled empires, financed wars, inspired philosophies, given rise
  to whole industries, and structured the daily rhythms of billions of people. After water, it is the most widely consumed beverage on earth. Its history is a history of trade and power, of religious ritual and domestic comfort, of
  agricultural ingenuity and brutal exploitation. To trace it is to travel from the misty highlands of southwest China to the drawing rooms of Georgian London, the plantations of Assam and Ceylon, the harbor of Boston, and the tea-stall
  counters of modern Delhi.

  [1mOrigins in Southwest China[0m

  The tea plant, [3mCamellia sinensis[0m, is native to the region where present-day Yunnan, Sichuan, Myanmar, and northern Laos and Vietnam meet. In these humid, forested uplands, wild tea trees grew for millennia before anyone thought to brew
  them. Exactly when people began to use the plant is lost to time, and the earliest chapters of tea history are bound up with legend.

  The best-known story credits the mythical emperor Shennong, the "Divine Farmer," who is said to have lived around 2737 BCE. According to the tale, Shennong tested herbs on himself to discover their medicinal properties, and on one
  occasion poisoned himself seventy-two times in a day. Leaves from a wild tree drifted into his boiling water, and the resulting infusion cured him. The story is charming, but it is a later invention, probably first written down more than
  two thousand years after its supposed date. Shennong belongs to myth, not to the historical record.

  What the evidence does suggest is that tea was first used by the peoples of the southwest, perhaps chewed, eaten as a vegetable, or boiled into a bitter medicinal broth, long before it became a refined drink. Archaeological finds have
  complicated the picture further. Remains of tea leaves have been identified in the tomb of Emperor Jing of Han, who died in 141 BCE, near Xi'an, which suggests the leaf was valued by the Han court by the second century BCE. Written
  references from the same era, though sometimes ambiguous because the Chinese character for tea was not yet standardized, point to tea as a drink in the Sichuan basin. A text from 59 BCE, a humorous contract by Wang Bao, mentions buying
  tea and preparing it, among the earliest reasonably secure references.

  Early tea was nothing like the drink we know. It was often boiled with onion, ginger, orange peel, or salt, producing something closer to a soup or a medicinal decoction. The leaves were frequently pressed into cakes for storage and
  transport, a form that would remain central to Chinese and Central Asian tea culture for more than a thousand years.

  [1mThe Tang Dynasty: Tea Becomes a Culture[0m

  Tea's transformation from regional remedy to national beverage occurred during the Tang dynasty (618–907). Several forces converged. Buddhism, which flourished under the Tang, valued tea as an aid to meditation, since monks could stay
  alert through long hours of sitting without breaking their prohibition on intoxicants. Monasteries cultivated tea on their hillsides and spread the habit along pilgrimage routes. At the same time, the dynasty's peace, its canal networks,
  and its cosmopolitan cities created the commercial conditions for a mass market.
