
  The Song Dynasty: Whisked Tea and Imperial Refinement

  Under the Song dynasty (960–1279), tea culture reached a remarkable level of sophistication. Powdered tea, whisked in a bowl until frothy, became the fashionable form. The finest tea cakes, stamped with dragons and phoenixes, were
  produced for the imperial court in the Beiyuan gardens of Fujian, and the competition to produce the most exquisite tribute tea became fierce. Aristocrats and literati held tea-tasting contests, called doucha, in which competitors judged
  the whiteness and persistence of the foam and the quality of the flavor. Black-glazed bowls from Fujian, with their lustrous streaks and spots, were prized because they showed off the white froth to best advantage.

  Emperor Huizong (r. 1100–1126), a gifted painter and calligrapher who proved a disastrous ruler, wrote a treatise on tea, the Daguan Chalun, in which he praised its power to bring tranquility and refinement. The cultural prestige of tea
  had by then reached the highest level of society, yet it also spread far down the social ladder. Teahouses proliferated in cities such as Kaifeng and Hangzhou, serving as places of conversation, business, entertainment, and sometimes less
  respectable pleasures.

  Tea also became a vital strategic commodity. The Song state, hard pressed by nomadic powers to the north and west, needed horses for its cavalry, and the steppe peoples and Tibetans wanted tea, which supplied vitamins and digestive relief
  to people subsisting on meat and dairy. The result was the Tea-Horse trade, a network of mountain trails through Sichuan and Yunnan into Tibet that would continue for centuries. Government monopolies regulated this exchange, and tea
  became, in effect, a form of currency in the frontier economy.
